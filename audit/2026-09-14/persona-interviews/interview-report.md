# Five simulated client interviews

Date: 14 September 2026

## What this is

Five sub-agents reviewed the current `newstyle` homepage as fictional Gippsland small-business owners. They are not real customers and their comments are not research findings. The exercise tests whether a time-poor person with low confidence in technology can understand the page without translating supplier language.

The five personas were:

1. Maya, owner-operator of a busy Traralgon cafe.
2. Mick, owner of an independent tyre and mechanical shop in Morwell.
3. Aisha, owner of a two-chair hair and beauty studio in Warragul.
4. Dave, a self-employed electrician in Sale with one apprentice.
5. Priya, owner of a small bookkeeping and tax practice in Bairnsdale.

They reviewed the homepage opening, service choices, enquiry section, 390px mobile opening and open mobile navigation. Those accepted screenshots are saved beside this report as `01-current-home-desktop.png` through `05-current-menu-mobile.png`. `06-kristi-reference-current.png` records the reference page used for the revision. Updated desktop and mobile evidence is saved as `07-updated-home-desktop.png` through `11-updated-menu-mobile.png`.

## What every persona agreed on

- The orange and violet arrive before the offer. The combination feels hot, loud or more like a creative agency than a calm local technology provider.
- The large type is easy to read and the hard outlines and shadows make actions obvious. Those parts should stay.
- The opening promise is too broad. People only understand the offer when they reach Websites, Business IT and Security.
- Technical and supplier language creates unnecessary work. `Built with Astro`, `UniFi`, `defined project`, `scope the result`, `project work` and `technology consulting` were repeatedly named.
- The 12Grapes project proves that Servo has completed real local work, but it mainly proves website design. It needs an outcome in plain language and less space before the service choices.
- The email-draft form is confusing. `Open email draft`, `Copy enquiry` and the explanation that nothing sends automatically create doubt about which action works.
- Local signals build trust. Gippsland, Rowan's name, a real phone number, phone hours, an agreed scope and one point of contact all helped.

## Interview summaries

### 1. Maya, cafe owner

Maya understood websites, computers and security, but could not tell whether Servo could help with the everyday things she knows: cafe Wi-Fi, business email, an online menu, a booking or payment account, and Google business details. She would inspect service types before agreeing to a call.

She wanted a calm background, three familiar starting choices and a shorter page. She would remove the blog from the homepage and move the local project below the service choices.

Simulated closing quote: "I think you can help, but I have to work too hard to figure out what I should ask you for."

### 2. Mick, tyre shop owner

Mick read the opening as a website-design business because the vineyard website dominates the first screen. He would look for computers and Wi-Fi, but `Project work` does not tell him where to go. He liked the short form and local details, then lost confidence when the form offered two ways to finish in another app.

He wanted the opening to name websites, computers and Wi-Fi, with a visible phone option and proof of business IT work. He would cut the blog and reduce the navigation.

Simulated closing quote: "I can tell you're local and probably capable, but I still can't tell quickly enough whether you're the person who'll fix my workshop computers."

### 3. Aisha, salon owner

Aisha liked the visual polish, readable type, service cards and manageable three-step process. She could not connect broad service labels to her domain, business email, Fresha booking link, salon Wi-Fi or staff accounts. A 30-minute call felt like too much commitment before price or fit was clear.

She wanted a cream left side and one coloured right side, a more concrete promise and fewer sections. She would remove `Built with Astro` and describe the 12Grapes result in customer terms.

Simulated closing quote: "If I can tell in ten seconds that Rowan will sort out my website, email and bookings without making me learn the technical bits, I'll make the enquiry."

### 4. Dave, electrician

Dave wanted a phone-first route through the page and examples involving working email, quoting, scheduling, invoicing, Wi-Fi and account recovery. The word `project` made him assume Servo only accepts planned work. The orange reminded him of a safety sign, and the quantity made it tiring rather than useful.

He wanted a visible tap-to-call option, three short navigation choices, one compact local example and the blog moved out of the homepage.

Simulated closing quote: "You look capable, but I'm too busy to translate all this into whether you can fix the problems I actually have."

### 5. Priya, bookkeeper

Priya saw real signs of a competent local operator, but the colour and website-led proof did not give her enough confidence to entrust Servo with staff accounts or systems containing client information. She wanted concrete evidence about access, backups, recovery and what happens after handover.

She would visit About before enquiring, then Security. She wanted fewer routes, one obvious enquiry action and a reminder not to include passwords or sensitive client information.

Simulated closing quote: "I can see that Rowan builds good websites, but I cannot yet tell whether I would trust Servo to protect my clients' information and keep my practice running."

## Screen-by-screen health

1. Homepage opening: poor. The type is strong, but colour and the project screenshot dominate a broad promise. Visitors cannot quickly map the page to a familiar business need.
2. Service choices: mixed. The three-category structure works, but the headings and descriptions still ask visitors to translate technology and project language.
3. Enquiry section: mixed. The field count is manageable and contact details help. The static email handoff creates doubt because two actions and several explanatory lines compete.
4. Mobile opening: poor. It reflows without clipping, but the large headline, full orange field, call button and project panel delay the practical choices.
5. Mobile navigation: mixed. Targets are easy to tap. The large overlay covers the page and presents more choices than these visitors need.

## Changes supported by the interviews

1. Use a warm neutral page and confine a strong accent to one purposeful side of the opening, following the current Kristi reference. Retain dark outlines, readable type and hard shadows.
2. Lead with the confirmed starter need: a website, business email and essential accounts, set up with the customer. Let visitors choose a familiar problem without diagnosing the technology.
3. Move the local project below the service choices. Replace framework detail with the business problem and useful result.
4. Reduce the main navigation to What we help with, How it works and Contact. Keep About and Blog reachable lower on the page and in the footer.
5. Remove the blog section from the homepage. Keep the blog itself and footer link.
6. Make the form's true static behaviour understandable in one sentence. Keep one primary action, demote the copy fallback and warn visitors not to include passwords or sensitive client information.

## Revision made from the feedback

- Replaced the full orange opening and violet project panel with a cream reading surface and one coral starter panel. Coral now marks the starter offer and selected work; mint marks trust, handover and contact.
- Rewrote the opening around the confirmed starter need: a website, business email and essential accounts, with a visible phone option.
- Put three plain-language problem choices before trust and portfolio proof. The local project now explains the customer outcome and no longer leads with its framework.
- Reduced the main navigation to three destinations and moved About and Blog to the lower page and footer.
- Shortened the process copy and removed the homepage blog section.
- Simplified the enquiry instructions, made the email action explicit, demoted the copy fallback and added a sensitive-information warning.

## Screen health after the revision

1. Homepage opening: good. The offer and next actions are visible in one screen, while the strong colour is confined to the starter panel.
2. Service choices: good. Three equal cards use familiar problems and include examples such as email, computers and Wi-Fi.
3. Enquiry section: good. Contact details, reply timing and the real email handoff are visible without competing instructions.
4. Mobile opening: good. The main promise, one service-choice action and tap-to-call appear before the starter panel.
5. Mobile navigation: good. The open menu contains three large targets and no secondary content routes.

## Evidence limits

The screenshots support comments about hierarchy, wording, colour, density and responsive layout. Browser checks can test links, focus and form behaviour, but the simulated interviews cannot establish what real customers understand, prefer or trust. The next useful validation would be five short sessions with actual small-business owners using their own phones.
