# Servo ICT audience

Status: working brief based on the owner's instructions, 14 September 2026. Customer interviews have not been conducted. This document records the intended audience, not measured customer behaviour.

Current visual direction: Concept 3, selected by the owner after reviewing the published Concept 1 implementation. The [Concept 3 implementation note](docs/design/concept-3-build/implementation.md) records the selected image and page sequence. The audience and starter-offer decisions below still apply.

## Who this is for

Small business owners with very little technical experience or confidence. Setting up an email account may already be difficult. They know their business; they should not need to learn technology vocabulary to decide whether Servo can help.

The primary audience is starting a business and needs the basics set up. They want Servo to handle the setup and show them the few things they need to know. Their leading website purchase priority is a clear, affordable price and a site that does the essentials well. These priorities were explicitly confirmed by the owner after the initial review. Existing businesses remain a possible secondary audience.

Write for the customer making the purchase, not for Servo's owner, another developer or an IT procurement team. Do not infer age, education, intelligence, industry, budget or device preference from low technical confidence.

## Working hypotheses to validate

A primary visitor may be thinking "I'm starting a business and need someone to get the basics working." The owner has confirmed that the main starter offer covers a website, business email and essential account setup. The specific accounts, quantities, setup tasks and ongoing costs remain to be defined. Repairing existing systems or replacing an existing website can remain secondary scenarios, but should not displace the confirmed startup audience in the main message.

Likely decision questions:

1. Do you help a business like mine with this particular problem?
2. What will I get, and what will be better when it is finished?
3. What will it cost now and later? Am I committing to a contract?
4. What do I need to provide or learn? Will someone help me gather it?
5. Who will I talk to, how long will it take, and what happens after I enquire?
6. Who handles changes or problems later, and what does that cost?

Affordable essentials and setup handled by Servo are confirmed priorities. The owner has not specified a customer budget ceiling or approved a new price. Showing the few necessary tasks does not mean expecting the customer to administer the whole setup. The method for later website changes and ongoing help remains to be defined.

## Writing and offer rules

- Lead with a recognisable problem and a concrete result. Use examples involving calls, enquiries, opening hours, services, prices, email, computers and Wi-Fi where relevant.
- Translate technical facts into useful outcomes. Explain the minimum necessary technical term next to the customer benefit if the term helps a decision.
- Do not require customers to diagnose their problem, select a framework, know their hosting provider or prepare a technical brief before contacting Servo.
- Describe the customer's effort in everyday actions. For example: a conversation about the business, sharing existing photos, and checking draft wording.
- Keep the full price, minimum term, total commitment, exclusions and ongoing responsibilities easy to find. Simpler language must not hide a material condition.
- Show relevant Servo client work and describe the business need and what was delivered. Famous brands using the same software are not evidence of Servo's results.
- Keep ownership, local availability, limits of support and the next contact step explicit.
- Make contact work for someone who does not know how to configure an email app. Review the current email-draft form against this requirement before changing it.
- Present a useful interactive feature only when it helps the customer's business. Do not assume every simple website needs a calculator or guided tool.
- Lead the main website offer with affordable essentials. Explain extra features separately when useful, without changing prices or inclusions until the offer is defined.
- Describe starting from scratch as well as improving an existing website. Do not assume the customer already has business accounts, website access, finished wording or photographs.
- Preserve respectful, adult language. Avoid both jargon and patronising simplification.

## Translate the benefit, then check the promise

| Internal topic | Customer question to answer | Evidence or delivery decision needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Astro framework | Will my website load quickly and work on a phone? | Test the finished site. A framework alone does not guarantee speed or quality. |
| Editing and deployment | Can I change my hours, prices or photos, and who does it? | Define the editing method, support arrangement, cost and realistic turnaround. |
| Reusable components | Can you get this built efficiently at a price I can afford? | Define and price a real offer. Do not infer cheapness from the technology. |
| Hosting and maintenance | Who keeps the site available and deals with problems? | State who is responsible, what care includes and what it costs. |
| Scope and handover | What is included, what do I need to do, and what happens when it is ready? | Explain the agreed work, training or help, ownership and aftercare. |
| Security tools | Will my business email, accounts and files be better protected? | Describe concrete setup work and recovery help without guaranteeing safety. |

## Review scorecard

For each section, reviewers independently score audience fit out of 10. Consider understanding, relevance, confidence and the next action. This is a judgement tool, not a customer survey or a validated usability measure.

- 9–10: immediately understandable, clearly useful and low effort to act on.
- 7–8: useful and mostly clear, with a small gap or unfamiliar term.
- 5–6: the point can be understood with effort, but value or expectations are unclear.
- 3–4: substantial confusion, irrelevant emphasis or too much work required.
- 1–2: likely to block understanding or action, or mainly serves a technical audience.

Each score needs a specific phrase or interaction as evidence, the useful part to keep, the likely point of confusion, and a proposed direction. An imagined customer reaction must be labelled as a hypothesis, never a real quote from a customer.

## Check before approving a page section

- Name the customer question this section answers.
- Check that the answer makes sense without knowing a product, framework or IT term.
- Explain the customer's effort and responsibility where they affect the decision.
- Keep costs, commitments and the main next step easy to find.
- Support claims with actual work, a defined service or a relevant check.
- Remove or move material that mainly reassures a technical supplier rather than this customer.
